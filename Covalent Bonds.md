## Fundamental Principles of Chemical Bonding

### Classification of Primary Chemical Bonds
* **Covalent Bonding**:
  * Occurs almost exclusively between non-metallic elements with relatively close electronegativity values.
  * Characterized by the sharing of valence electron pairs between atomic nuclei.
  * Leads to the formation of discrete molecular structures or continuous covalent network solids.
* **Ionic Bonding**:
  * Occurs typically between metallic elements (low ionization energy) and non-metallic elements (high electron affinity).
  * Involves the complete transfer of one or more valence electrons from the metal atom to the non-metal atom.
  * Results in electrostatic attraction between positively charged cations and negatively charged anions within an extended crystal lattice.
* **Metallic Bonding**:
  * Occurs among metallic elements.
  * Characterized by a "sea of delocalized electrons" surrounding fixed, positively charged metallic cations.
  * Accounts for physical properties such as electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, malleability, and ductility.

## Atomic Structure, Valence Shells, and Electron Configurations

### Principal Energy Levels and Quantum Shells
* **Principal Quantum Number ($n$)**:
  * Indicates the primary energy level or shell surrounding the atomic nucleus.
  * As $n$ increases, the average distance of the electron from the nucleus increases, alongside an increase in total energy.
* **Sub-shells ($s$, $p$, $d$, $f$)**:
  * Each principal energy level consists of one or more sub-shells corresponding to orbital angular momentum.
  * Sub-shell types designate orbital geometry:
    * $s$ sub-shell: $1$ spherical orbital (maximum $2$ electrons).
    * $p$ sub-shell: $3$ dumbbell-shaped orbitals (maximum $6$ electrons).
    * $d$ sub-shell: $5$ complex orbitals (maximum $10$ electrons).
    * $f$ sub-shell: $7$ complex orbitals (maximum $14$ electrons).

### Maximum Electron Capacities per Energy Level
* The maximum electron capacity of any given principal energy level $n$ is defined mathematically by the quantum relation:
  $$\text{Maximum Electrons} = 2n^2$$
* Breakdown of sub-shell distributions across energy levels:
  * **Level $n = 1$**:
    * Sub-shells present: $s$
    * Number of orbitals by sub-shell: $1$
    * Maximum sub-shell electron capacities: $2$
    * Total maximum capacity: $2(1)^2 = 2$ electrons.
  * **Level $n = 2$**:
    * Sub-shells present: $s$, $p$
    * Number of orbitals by sub-shell: $1$ ($s$), $3$ ($p$)
    * Maximum sub-shell electron capacities: $2$ ($s$), $6$ ($p$)
    * Total maximum capacity: $2(2)^2 = 8$ electrons.
  * **Level $n = 3$**:
    * Sub-shells present: $s$, $p$, $d$
    * Number of orbitals by sub-shell: $1$ ($s$), $3$ ($p$), $5$ ($d$)
    * Maximum sub-shell electron capacities: $2$ ($s$), $6$ ($p$), $10$ ($d$)
    * Total maximum capacity: $2(3)^2 = 18$ electrons.
  * **Level $n = 4$**:
    * Sub-shells present: $s$, $p$, $d$, $f$
    * Number of orbitals by sub-shell: $1$ ($s$), $3$ ($p$), $5$ ($d$), $7$ ($f$)
    * Maximum sub-shell electron capacities: $2$ ($s$), $6$ ($p$), $10$ ($d$), $14$ ($f$)
    * Total maximum capacity: $2(4)^2 = 32$ electrons.

## Valence Electrons and Periodic Trends

### Definition and Chemical Role
* **Valence Electrons**:
  * Defined as electrons located in the outermost principal shell ($n$) of a main-group atom.
  * Specifically consist of the highest energy $s$ and $p$ electrons.
  * Determine the chemical reactivity, bonding capacity (valency), and oxidation states of elements.
* **Core Electrons**:
  * Inner-shell electrons situated below the highest principal energy level.
  * Serve to shield valence electrons from the full nuclear charge ($Z$).

### Periodic Relationships
* Elements residing within the same main group (column) on the periodic table possess identical valence electron configurations.
* Consequently, elements within a group exhibit similar chemical behavior, valence capabilities, and bonding modes.
* **Group Trends**:
  * Group 1 (Alkali Metals): $1$ valence electron ($ns^1$).
  * Group 2 (Alkaline Earth Metals): $2$ valence electrons ($ns^2$).
  * Group 14 (Carbon Family): $4$ valence electrons ($ns^2 np^2$).
  * Group 15 (Pnictogens): $5$ valence electrons ($ns^2 np^3$).
  * Group 16 (Chalcogens): $6$ valence electrons ($ns^2 np^4$).
  * Group 17 (Halogens): $7$ valence electrons ($ns^2 np^5$).
  * Group 18 (Noble Gases): $8$ valence electrons ($ns^2 np^6$, excluding Helium which has $2$).

## The Octet Rule and Chemical Stability

### Principles of Stability
* **Octet Rule**:
  * States that main-group atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons until they are surrounded by $8$ valence electrons.
  * An octet corresponds to a filled outermost $s$ and $p$ sub-shell ($ns^2 np^6$).
  * Emulates the stable, low-energy electron configuration of noble gases.
* **Exceptions to the Octet Rule**:
  * **Duet Rule**: Hydrogen ($H$) and Helium ($He$) achieve maximum stability with $2$ valence electrons ($1s^2$).
  * **Incomplete Octets**: Elements like Beryllium ($Be$) and Boron ($B$) often form stable covalent compounds with fewer than $8$ valence electrons (e.g., $BF_3$ with $6$ electrons).
  * **Expanded Octets**: Elements in Period 3 and below (e.g., Phosphorus, Sulfur, Chlorine) can utilize available empty $d$ orbitals to accommodate more than $8$ valence electrons (e.g., $PF_5$, $SF_6$).
  * **Odd-Electron Molecules**: Species containing an odd number of total valence electrons (free radicals such as $NO$) cannot satisfy the octet rule for every atom.

## Covalent Structure Construction and Lewis Formulas

### Systematic Rules for Drawing Lewis Structures
1. **Determine the Central Atom**:
   * Identify the central atom, which is typically the least electronegative element (excluding Hydrogen, which is strictly terminal).
   * Carbon ($C$) is predominantly central because it forms up to $4$ covalent bonds.
2. **Calculate Total Valence Electrons**:
   * Sum the valence electrons from all constituent atoms.
   * Adjust for overall molecular charge: add electrons for net negative charges (anions) and subtract electrons for net positive charges (cations).
3. **Form Skeleton Structure**:
   * Connect each peripheral (terminal) atom to the central atom using a single covalent bond ($1$ electron pair / shared pair).
4. **Distribute Remaining Electrons**:
   * Complete the octets (or duets for $H$) of all outer/peripheral atoms first by placing unshared electron pairs (lone pairs).
   * Assign any leftover electrons directly to the central atom as lone pairs.
5. **Optimize Multiple Bonds (If Necessary)**:
   * If the central atom lacks an octet after distributing all available electrons, convert lone pairs from peripheral atoms into double or triple covalent bonds attached to the central atom.

### Detailed Case Study: Chloromethane ($CH_3Cl$)
* **Central Atom Selection**:
  * Carbon ($C$) acts as the central atom as it requires $4$ covalent bonds to achieve a full octet.
* **Valence Electron Count Calculation**:
  * Carbon ($C$): $1 \times 4 = 4$ electrons
  * Hydrogen ($H$): $3 \times 1 = 3$ electrons
  * Chlorine ($Cl$): $1 \times 7 = 7$ electrons
  * Total available valence electrons:
    $$\text{Total} = 4 + (3 \times 1) + 7 = 14 \text{ valence electrons}$$
* **Structural Assembly**:
  * Connect three $H$ atoms and one $Cl$ atom to central $C$ via single covalent bonds ($4$ bonds total $\times 2$ electrons per bond = $8$ bonding electrons used).
  * Remaining non-bonding electrons: $14 - 8 = 6$ valence electrons.
  * Place the remaining $6$ electrons as $3$ lone pairs on the terminal Chlorine ($Cl$) atom.
  * Verify stability:
    * Hydrogen atoms satisfy the duet rule ($2$ electrons each via single bonds).
    * Carbon satisfies the octet rule ($8$ bonding electrons from $4$ single bonds).
    * Chlorine satisfies the octet rule ($2$ bonding electrons + $6$ non-bonding electrons = $8$ electrons total).

## Rules for Writing Molecular Formulas

### Subscript Conventions
* Use numerical subscripts immediately following elemental symbols to denote the exact count of each atom present in a single discrete molecule.
* **Strict Negation Rule**:
  * **DO NOT EVER USE A SUBSCRIPT OF 1**.
  * A single atom of an element is implicitly designated by the chemical symbol alone without subscript accompaniment (e.g., $H_2O$ indicates two $H$ and one $O$, not $H_2O_1$).

## Advanced Covalent Bonding Concepts

### Coordinate (Dative) Covalent Bonding
* **Definition**:
  * A special form of covalent bond in which both shared bonding electrons are contributed by a single donor atom.
* **Mechanisms and Conditions**:
  * Requires a donor atom possessing an available lone pair of electrons.
  * Requires an acceptor atom possessing an empty valence orbital capable of receiving the electron pair.
  * Once formed, a coordinate covalent bond is identical in strength, length, and behavior to a standard covalent bond.
* **Representative Examples**:
  * Ammonium ion ($NH_4^+$): The lone pair from ammonia ($NH_3$) coordinates to an incoming electron-deficient hydrogen ion ($H^+$).
  * Hydronium ion ($H_3O^+$): Formed when a lone pair from water ($H_2O$) bonds to a proton ($H^+$).

## VSEPR Theory and Molecular Geometry

### Core Tenets of VSEPR Theory
* **Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR)**:
  * Molecular geometries are predicted based on the electrostatic repulsion between electron pairs in the valence shell of a central atom.
  * Electron pairs (both bonding pairs and lone pairs) arrange themselves spatially around a central atom to maximize separation distance and minimize inter-electronic repulsion.

### Step-by-Step VSEPR Structural Prediction
1. **Draw the Correct Lewis Structure**:
   * Determine total valence count, draw skeletal connections, and complete octets across peripheral and central atoms.
2. **Count Electron Domains Around Central Atom**:
   * Treat single bonds, double bonds, triple bonds, and lone pairs each as a single "electron domain" (or sterically active region).
3. **Determine Electron-Domain Geometry**:
   * Arrange total electron domains as far apart in $3D$ space as possible to minimize potential energy.
4. **Identify Molecular Geometry (Shape)**:
   * Factor in differences between bonding pairs and non-bonding (lone) pairs.
   * Lone pairs exert greater repulsive forces than bonding pairs, slightly compressing adjacent bond angles.
   * Name the final structural geometry based solely on the physical positions of the atomic nuclei.

### Common Molecular Geometries and Ideal Bond Angles
* **$2$ Electron Domains**:
  * Electron Geometry: Linear
  * $0$ Lone Pairs: **Linear** (Ideal Bond Angle: $180^\circ$)
* **$3$ Electron Domains**:
  * Electron Geometry: Trigonal Planar
  * $0$ Lone Pairs: **Trigonal Planar** (Ideal Bond Angle: $120^\circ$)
  * $1$ Lone Pair: **Bent** / Angular (Ideal Bond Angle: $< 120^\circ$)
* **$4$ Electron Domains**:
  * Electron Geometry: Tetrahedral
  * $0$ Lone Pairs: **Tetrahedral** (Ideal Bond Angle: $109.5^\circ$)
  * $1$ Lone Pair: **Trigonal Pyramidal** (Ideal Bond Angle: $\approx 107^\circ$)
  * $2$ Lone Pairs: **Bent** / Angular (Ideal Bond Angle: $\approx 104.5^\circ$)
* **$5$ Electron Domains**:
  * Electron Geometry: Trigonal Bipyramidal
  * $0$ Lone Pairs: **Trigonal Bipyramidal** (Ideal Bond Angles: $90^\circ$ axial, $120^\circ$ equatorial)
  * $1$ Lone Pair: **Seesaw**
  * $2$ Lone Pairs: **T-Shaped**
  * $3$ Lone Pairs: **Linear**
* **$6$ Electron Domains**:
  * Electron Geometry: Octahedral
  * $0$ Lone Pairs: **Octahedral** (Ideal Bond Angle: $90^\circ$)
  * $1$ Lone Pair: **Square Pyramidal**
  * $2$ Lone Pairs: **Square Planar**
