# Subatomic Particles
## Protons ($p^+$)
- In nucleus
- Charge of +1
- Mass of 1 amu
## Neutrons ($n^0$)
- In nucleus
- Charge of 0
-  Mass of 1 amu
## Electrons ($e^+$)
- In the electron cloud around the nucleus
- Charge of -1
- Mass of $\frac{1}{1823} \approx 0$
# Atoms
## Element symbol
- An element symbol is a 1-2 letter abbreviation of the element’s name (not always in English)
## Atomic number ($Z$)
- Each element has a unique # of protons (the atomic number)
- The same # of electrons balances this
## Mass number ($A$)
- The mass number of an isotope is the number of protons & neutrons in an isotope combined.
## Atomic mass
- The atomic mass of an element is the average mass of all stable (non-radioactive) isotopes of the element, weighted by abundance
- i.e., the mass of the average atom of that element
- i.e., $m_1*a_1+m_2*a_2 ... m_x*a_x$ where $a$ is the abundance of a given isotope & $m$ is the mass of the isotope.
- If there are no stable isotopes, the mass of the isotope with the longest half-life is used.
# Radioactive decay
## Alpha decay
- In alpha decay, the "parent" (starting) isotope produces a "daughter" isotope with 2 fewer protons & 2 fewer neutrons, as well as a helium-4 atom.
  - $\begin{array}{l} A \\ Z \end{array}\mathrm{X} \rightarrow \begin{array}{l} A-4 \\ Z-2 \end{array}\mathrm{Y} + \begin{array}{l} 4 \\ 2 \end{array}\mathrm{He}$
## Beta decay
- In beta decay, the "parent" (starting) isotope produces a "daughter" isotope with one more proton, an electron, and an electron antineutrino.
  - $\begin{array}{l} A \\ Z \end{array} \text{X} \rightarrow \begin{array}{l} A \\ Z+1 \end{array} \text{Y} + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e$
## Gamma emission
- In gamma emission, the "parent" (starting) isotope produces a "daughter" isotope and a high-energy photon. The number of protons and neutrons does not change.
  - $\begin{array}{l} A \\ Z \end{array}\mathrm{X}^* \rightarrow \begin{array}{l} A \\ Z \end{array}\mathrm{X} + \gamma$
## Positron emission
- In positron emission, the "parent" (starting) isotope produces a "daughter" isotope with one fewer proton, a positron, and an electron neutrino.
  - $\begin{array}{l} A \\ Z \end{array}\mathrm{X} \rightarrow \begin{array}{l} A \\ Z-1 \end{array}\mathrm{Y} + e^+ + \nu_e$
## Electron capture
- In electron capture, the "parent" (starting) isotope captures an electron from its surroundings. A proton is converted into a neutron, producing a daughter isotope with one fewer proton and an electron neutrino.
  - $\begin{array}{l} A \\ Z \end{array}\mathrm{X} + e^- \rightarrow \begin{array}{l} A \\ Z-1 \end{array}\mathrm{Y} + \nu_e$
## Neutron emission
- In neutron emission, the "parent" (starting) isotope produces a "daughter" isotope and a neutron. The mass number decreases by 1, while the atomic number stays the same.
  - $\begin{array}{l} A \\ Z \end{array}\mathrm{X} \rightarrow \begin{array}{l} A-1 \\ Z \end{array}\mathrm{Y} + \begin{array}{l} 1 \\ 0 \end{array}\mathrm{n}$
# Half-life
- The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the amount of time required for half of a sample's radioactive nuclei to decay.
- After each half-life, the amount of the original isotope is cut in half.
- The amount remaining after $n$ half-lives is:
  - $\frac{N}{N_0} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n$
- For example, after 1 half-life, 50% remains; after 2 half-lives, 25% remains; after 3 half-lives, 12.5% remains.
# Electron levels
- Electrons exist in a cloud surrounding the nucleus, organized into different energy levels.
- Electrons are attracted to the positively charged protons in the nucleus.
- Electrons are repelled by other negatively charged electrons.
- Electrons are most stable when they occupy the lowest energy state possible.

# Electron orbitals
- Orbitals are regions within the electron cloud where electrons are likely to be found.
- Each type of orbital can hold a specific maximum number of electrons:
  - **s:** 2 electrons
  - **p:** 6 electrons
  - **d:** 10 electrons
  - **f:** 14 electrons
- Electrons generally occupy the lowest-energy orbitals available first.
