# Unit 03: Covalent Bonding

## Types of Chemical Bonds

- **Covalent bonds** form between nonmetal atoms. The atoms share valence electrons, allowing each atom to achieve a more stable outer-shell arrangement.
- **Ionic bonds** form between a metal and a nonmetal. In the model used here, the metal loses one or more electrons and becomes a positively charged **cation**, while the nonmetal gains electrons and becomes a negatively charged **anion**. The oppositely charged ions attract.
- **Metallic bonds** occur between metal atoms.

The key distinction is what happens to the electrons: covalent bonding involves sharing, while the introductory ionic model involves electron transfer. These categories describe predominant bonding behavior; the electronegativity section below shows why bonding can fall along a continuum.

## Covalent Bonding and the Octet Rule

- Covalent compounds are composed of nonmetals.
- In covalent bonding, atoms share electrons so that each atom tends toward a stable arrangement of valence electrons.
- The **octet rule** is the pattern in which many atoms form bonds until they have eight electrons in their outer occupied shell.
- **Hydrogen is an exception:** its first shell can hold only two electrons, so hydrogen follows a duet pattern rather than an octet.
- Noble gases are generally unreactive because their outer electron shells are already complete.
- The term **molecule** is used for a group of atoms joined by covalent bonds.
- Molecular models are representations, not the objects themselves. A model can make some features easier to see while leaving other features out. Methane, CH₄, is one example used to discuss the strengths and limitations of models.

The octet rule is a useful guide for drawing introductory Lewis structures, but it is a pattern rather than a universal rule. The slides include NO₂ as an octet-rule exception.

## Lewis Structures

- **Lewis structures** represent valence electrons and show how those electrons are arranged around the atoms in a molecule.
- Dots represent valence electrons. A pair of electrons shared between two atoms represents a covalent bond.
- A shared pair can be drawn as two dots (`:`) or as a line (`—`). One line therefore represents one shared pair, or two bonding electrons.
- Lewis structures connect bonding patterns to the stability associated with filled outer shells.

### Constructing a Lewis structure

1. **Count the available valence electrons.** Add the valence-electron contributions from every atom in the formula.
2. **Choose a central atom.** Connect the other atoms to it. In the slide example CH₃Cl, carbon is central because it forms the most bonds.
3. **Connect the atoms with single bonds.** Each bond uses one pair of electrons.
4. **Place the remaining electrons.** Complete the outer shells of atoms other than hydrogen first; hydrogen needs two electrons, not eight.
5. **Check the structure.** Confirm that the number of electrons drawn matches the total available and that the bonding pattern is consistent with the usual covalent bond counts below.

- **Example — CH₃Cl:** the total number of valence electrons is $4 + 3(1) + 7 = 14$. Carbon contributes 4, the three hydrogen atoms contribute 3 altogether, and chlorine contributes 7. The structure must account for all 14 electrons.
- When checking a Lewis structure, distinguish **bonding pairs** (shared between atoms) from **lone pairs** (assigned to one atom and not shown as a bond).

### Typical covalent bond counts (HONC rule)

The HONC rule is a memory aid for the common number of covalent bonds formed by several nonmetals. It is a quick check for introductory Lewis structures, not a claim that every compound must follow only one arrangement.

- **H and halogens:** usually 1 covalent bond.
- **O and S:** usually 2 covalent bonds. Common patterns include two single bonds or one double bond.
- **N and P:** usually 3 covalent bonds. Patterns can include three single bonds, one double bond plus one single bond, or one triple bond.
- **C and Si:** usually 4 covalent bonds. Patterns can include four single bonds, two double bonds, one triple bond plus one single bond, or one double bond plus two single bonds.

A double bond contains two shared electron pairs; a triple bond contains three. When using the HONC pattern, check both the number of bonds and the total valence-electron count.

## Bond Dissociation Energy, Bond Length, and Bond Order

- **Bond dissociation energy** is the energy required to break a covalent bond between two atoms.
- **Bond length** is the distance between the bonded nuclei.
- **Bond order** distinguishes a single, double, or triple bond: single = 1, double = 2, triple = 3.
- For the bond examples shown in the slides, a higher bond order corresponds to a shorter bond length and generally a greater bond energy.
- The data show this pattern for C–C, C=C, and C≡C: the listed length decreases from 154 pm to 134 pm to 120 pm, while the listed energy increases from 346 kJ/mol to 612 kJ/mol to 835 kJ/mol.

## Naming Binary Covalent Compounds

- **Binary covalent compounds** contain two different nonmetals.
- Prefixes indicate how many atoms of each element appear in the molecular formula.
- The name of the first element keeps its element name. The second element is named with an **-ide** ending.
- Prefixes are important because two nonmetals can form compounds in different atom ratios.

### Number prefixes

- 1 — mono-
- 2 — di-
- 3 — tri-
- 4 — tetra-
- 5 — penta-
- 6 — hexa-
- 7 — hepta-
- 8 — octa-
- 9 — nona-
- 10 — deca-

### Naming procedure

1. **Name the first nonmetal.**
   - Do not use **mono-** when there is only one atom of the first element.
   - If there is more than one atom of the first element, use the appropriate prefix.
2. **Name the second nonmetal.**
   - Use a prefix to show the number of atoms, including **mono-** when there is one atom.
   - Change the end of the element name to **-ide**.
3. **When converting a name to a formula,** use the prefixes as subscripts. Do not write a subscript 1.

### Examples from the slides

- CO₂ — **carbon dioxide**: one carbon (no mono- on the first element) and two oxygens (di-).
- N₂O₅ — **dinitrogen pentoxide**: two nitrogen atoms and five oxygen atoms.
- CCl₄ — **carbon tetrachloride**: one carbon and four chlorine atoms.
- PCl₃ — **phosphorus trichloride**: one phosphorus and three chlorine atoms.
- SO₂ — **sulfur dioxide**: one sulfur and two oxygen atoms.
- S₂Br₆ — **disulfur hexabromide**: two sulfur atoms and six bromine atoms.
- BH₃ — **boron trihydride**: one boron and three hydrogen atoms.

The prefixes describe atom counts in a molecule; they are not charges. Apply the naming procedure in order so the first-element mono- rule is not accidentally applied to the second element.

## Electronegativity and Bond Polarity

- **Electronegativity (EN)** is a unitless calculated value estimating how strongly an atom attracts bonding, or shared valence, electrons.
- Fluorine has the greatest electronegativity in the scale shown by the slides. Its value is given as 3.98 and rounded to 4.0 in an example.
- When two bonded atoms have different electronegativities, they attract the shared electrons by different amounts. This unequal sharing is the basis of bond polarity.

### Periodic trends

- **Across a period:** electronegativity generally increases. The slides explain this in terms of increasing nuclear attraction and decreasing atomic radius.
- **Down a group:** electronegativity generally decreases or may remain unchanged. Added electron shells place valence electrons farther from the nucleus, reducing the attraction they experience.
- Across a period, electrons are added to subshell orbitals of similar energy while nuclear attraction increases, pulling electrons closer.
- Down a group, additional shells increase the distance between the nucleus and the valence electrons. The slides relate the weaker attraction to the inverse-square relationship with distance.\n\n![Conceptual diagram of how electrostatic attraction changes with distance](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/donbt256/Chemistry/main/diagrams/covalent/inverse-square-distance.svg)
- **Atomic radius** is defined in the slides as half the distance between the nuclei in a covalently bonded diatomic molecule.
- Atomic radius generally decreases across a period and increases down a group.\n\n![Schematic atomic-radius trends across a period and down a group](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/donbt256/Chemistry/main/diagrams/covalent/atomic-radius-trends.svg)\n\nThe slides also connect the effect of distance to an inverse-square relationship:

### Using electronegativity difference

Calculate the absolute difference between the two atoms' electronegativities:

$[
\Delta EN = |EN_1 - EN_2|
]$

The slides give these guidelines for predicting the predominant bond type:

- **$\Delta EN < 0.4$ — generally nonpolar covalent:** electrons are shared approximately equally.
- **$\Delta EN$ from 0.4 to 1.7 — generally polar covalent:** electrons are shared unequally.
- **$\Delta EN > 1.7$ — generally ionic:** the bonding is predominantly associated with electron transfer and attraction between ions.

These boundaries are guidelines, not absolute rules. The slides specifically note HF as polar covalent even though its listed electronegativity difference is 1.78. Do not treat a cutoff as a rule that overrides the examples or the overall bonding context.

### Examples from the slides

- **Al–F:** $|4.0 - 1.6| = 2.4$ — predominantly ionic by the guideline.
- **C–O:** $|3.4 - 2.6| = 0.8$ — polar covalent.
- **Si–P:** $|2.2 - 1.9| = 0.3$ — nonpolar covalent.
- **Na–Cl:** $|3.2 - 0.9| = 2.3$ — predominantly ionic.
- Other polar bonds listed include H–O (2.20 vs. 3.44), H–Cl (2.20 vs. 3.16), and C–N (2.55 vs. 3.04).

When working an example, subtract the smaller EN from the larger EN or use the absolute-value expression so the difference is not negative. Then compare the result with the slide's approximate ranges.

## Coordinate Covalent (Dative) Bonds

- A **coordinate covalent bond** (also called a **dative bond**) is a covalent bond in which one atom supplies both electrons in the shared bonding pair.
- This differs from the usual introductory picture of a covalent bond, where each bonded atom contributes one electron to the shared pair.
- The completed bond is still covalent: the defining feature is where the bonding pair came from when the bond formed.

### Examples listed in the slides

- CO — carbon monoxide
- NH₄⁺ — ammonium
- SO₂ — sulfur dioxide
- SO₃ — sulfur trioxide
- NO₂ — nitrogen dioxide; noted in the slides as an octet-rule exception
- N₂O — nitrous oxide

In the ammonium illustration, hydrogen is introduced as an ion and nitrogen supplies the electron pair for the new bond. In the oxygen example, hydrogen is introduced as an ion and oxygen supplies the bonding electrons. These examples illustrate the defining idea: both electrons in the new bond are donated by one atom.

## Resonance

- **Resonance** occurs when more than one valid Lewis structure can be drawn for the same molecule.
- The different drawings represent alternative electron arrangements for the same molecular framework; they are not separate molecules rapidly switching back and forth.
- The slides describe the actual structure as an **average of its resonance structures**.
- **Ozone, O₃,** is an example of resonance in the slides.
- In **benzene, C₆H₆,** the inner electrons are represented as a ring.\n\n![Simplified benzene resonance drawings and ring shorthand](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/donbt256/Chemistry/main/diagrams/covalent/benzene-resonance.svg)

Resonance is useful when a single Lewis structure does not fully represent the electron arrangement described for a molecule. Keep the molecular identity the same when comparing its resonance structures.

## VSEPR and Molecular Geometry

- **VSEPR** stands for **Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion**.
- The model predicts the arrangement around a central atom by placing electron pairs as far apart as possible, minimizing repulsions between them.
- Geometry depends on both the number of electron domains around the central atom and how many of those domains are lone pairs.
- Molecular models simplify three-dimensional structures. As the slides emphasize, understand what a model represents as well as its assumptions and limitations.\n\n![Simplified VSEPR molecular geometry gallery](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/donbt256/Chemistry/main/diagrams/covalent/vsepr-geometries.svg)

### Predicting molecular geometry

1. Draw the Lewis structure.
2. Identify the electron pairs or domains around the central atom.
3. Arrange the electron domains as far apart as possible.
4. Determine the positions of the atoms from the shared pairs.
5. Name the molecular geometry based on the atom positions, taking lone pairs into account.

- **Steric number** is the number of atoms bonded to the central atom plus the number of lone pairs on that central atom.
- The slides give sulfur tetrafluoride as an example with steric number 5.
- Electron-domain arrangement and molecular geometry are related, but the name of the molecular geometry describes the positions of atoms, not lone pairs themselves.
- For this reason, the same steric number can lead to different molecular geometries when the number of lone pairs changes.

## Bond Polarity vs. Molecular Polarity

- **Bond polarity** concerns the electron sharing within one bond. A bond between atoms with different electronegativities can have an unequal electron distribution and a bond dipole.
- **Molecular polarity** depends on the molecule as a whole, including the arrangement of its bonds in three-dimensional space.
- A molecule can contain polar bonds and still be nonpolar overall if its geometry causes the individual bond dipoles to cancel.

### Example: carbon dioxide, CO₂

- Each C=O bond is polar: the slides give $EN_C = 2.55$ and $EN_O = 3.44$, for a difference of $0.89$.
- CO₂ has a **linear** geometry.
- The two polar C=O bonds point in opposite directions, so their effects cancel in the overall molecule.
- Therefore, CO₂ is presented as an example of a molecule with polar bonds but no overall molecular dipole.

When deciding whether a molecule is polar, do not stop after identifying polar bonds. First identify the geometry, then consider whether the bond dipoles cancel or leave a net dipole.
