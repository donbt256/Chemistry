# Unit 03: Covalent Bonding

## Types of Chemical Bonds
- **Covalent:** between two nonmetals; valence electrons are shared
- **Ionic:** between a metal and a nonmetal; the metal (cation) donates electron(s) to the nonmetal (anion)
- **Metallic:** between two metals

## Covalent Bonding and the Octet Rule
- Covalent compounds involve atoms of nonmetals only
- Covalent compounds tend to form so that each atom has an octet in its highest occupied energy level by sharing electrons
- Hydrogen is an exception to the octet pattern; its first shell holds 2 electrons
- Noble gases are generally unreactive because their outer shell is complete
- The term **molecule** is used for covalent bonding
- Models of molecules (including methane, CH₄) represent aspects of reality, but each model has strengths and limitations

## Lewis Structures
![Lewis dot notation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/donbt256/Chemistry/main/diagrams/electrons/lewis-dot-notation.svg)

- Lewis structures show how valence electrons are arranged among atoms in a molecule
- They reflect the relationship between stability and noble-gas electron configurations
- A shared pair of electrons is a covalent bond; represent it with two dots (:) or a line (—)
- To construct a Lewis structure:
  1. Add the available valence electrons for all atoms.
  2. Choose a central atom; in CH₃Cl, carbon is central because it forms the most bonds.
  3. Connect peripheral atoms to the central atom with shared electron pairs.
  4. Place remaining electrons to complete octets on atoms other than hydrogen.
- Example: CH₃Cl has $4 + 3(1) + 7 = 14$ available valence electrons

### Typical covalent bond counts (HONC rule)
- **H and halogens:** 1 covalent bond
- **O and S:** usually 2 covalent bonds — two single bonds or one double bond
- **N and P:** usually 3 covalent bonds — three single bonds, one double plus one single, or one triple bond
- **C and Si:** usually 4 covalent bonds — four single bonds, two double bonds, one triple plus one single, or one double plus two singles

- Binary covalent compounds contain two nonmetals

| 5 | penta- |
| 6 | hexa- |
| 7 | hepta- |
| 8 | octa- |
| 9 | nona- |
| 10 | deca- |

### Naming procedure
1. Name the first nonmetal.
   - Do **not** use mono- if there is only one atom of the first element.
   - Use the appropriate prefix if there is more than one.
2. Name the second nonmetal with a prefix and change its ending to **-ide**.
   - Always use a prefix for the second element, including mono- when appropriate.
- Examples:
  - CO₂ — carbon dioxide
  - N₂O₅ — dinitrogen pentoxide
  - CCl₄ — carbon tetrachloride
  - PCl₃ — phosphorus trichloride
  - SO₂ — sulfur dioxide
  - S₂Br₆ — disulfur hexabromide
  - BH₃ — boron trihydride
- When writing molecular formulas from names, use prefixes as subscripts and never write a subscript 1.

## Electronegativity and Bond Polarity
- **Electronegativity:** unitless calculated value estimating how strongly an atom attracts bonding (valence) electrons
- Fluorine has the greatest electronegativity; the slide gives 3.98 (rounded to 4.0 in an example)
- Electronegativity generally:
  - **Increases across a period** as effective nuclear attraction increases and atomic radius decreases
  - **Decreases or remains unchanged down a group** as additional shells increase atomic radius and distance from the nucleus
- Across a period, electrons are added to subshell orbitals of similar energy; increasing nuclear attraction pulls electrons closer
- Down a group, added shells place valence electrons farther from the nucleus; attraction decreases with distance (inverse-square relationship)
- **Atomic radius:** half the distance between nuclei in a covalently bonded diatomic molecule
  - Decreases across a period
  - Increases down a group
- Higher electronegativity means an atom attracts bonding electrons more strongly; lower electronegativity means it attracts them less strongly

### Using electronegativity difference
- Calculate $\Delta EN = |EN_1 - EN_2|$
- Use these slide guidelines to predict the predominant bond type:
  - **$\Delta EN < 0.4$:** generally nonpolar covalent; electrons are shared approximately equally
  - **$0.4$ to $1.7$:** polar covalent; electrons are shared unequally
  - **$\Delta EN > 1.7$:** ionic
- These are guidelines, not absolute rules; the slides note HF as a polar covalent bond despite an electronegativity difference of 1.78
- Examples from the slides:
  - Al–F: $|4.0 - 1.6| = 2.4$ — predominantly ionic
  - C–O: $|3.4 - 2.6| = 0.8$ — polar covalent
  - Si–P: $|2.2 - 1.9| = 0.3$ — nonpolar covalent
  - Na–Cl: $|3.2 - 0.9| = 2.3$ — predominantly ionic
- Examples of polar bonds given: H–O (2.20 vs. 3.44), H–Cl (2.20 vs. 3.16), and C–N (2.55 vs. 3.04)

## Coordinate Covalent (Dative) Bonds
- A covalent bond in which one atom supplies both bonding electrons
- Examples listed in the slides:
  - CO — carbon monoxide
  - NH₄⁺ — ammonium
  - SO₂ — sulfur dioxide
  - SO₃ — sulfur trioxide
  - NO₂ — nitrogen dioxide (noted as an octet-rule exception)
  - N₂O — nitrous oxide
- In the slide's ammonium illustration, hydrogen is introduced as an ion and nitrogen supplies the electron pair for the new bond
- In the slide's oxygen example, hydrogen is introduced as an ion and oxygen supplies the bonding electrons

## Resonance
- **Resonance** occurs when more than one valid Lewis structure can be drawn for the same molecule
- Example: ozone, O₃
- The actual structure is described as an average of its resonance structures
- In benzene, C₆H₆, the inner electrons are represented as a ring

## VSEPR and Molecular Geometry
- **VSEPR:** Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion model
- The arrangement around a central atom is principally determined by minimizing repulsions between electron pairs
- Molecular models are simplifications, not reality; understand their assumptions and limitations

### Predicting molecular geometry
1. Draw the Lewis structure.
2. Arrange electron pairs as far apart as possible.
3. Determine the positions of atoms from how electron pairs are shared.
4. Name the molecular geometry from the atom positions.
- **Steric number:** number of atoms bonded to the central atom plus the number of lone pairs on the central atom
- Example from the slides: sulfur tetrafluoride has steric number 5
- Geometry depends on both steric number and the number of lone pairs on the central atom.

## Bond Polarity vs. Molecular Polarity
- A nonpolar covalent bond has an electronegativity difference below about 0.4; electrons are shared equally or nearly equally and the bond does not form a dipole
- Molecular geometry can make a molecule nonpolar even when it contains polar bonds
- Example: CO₂ has polar C=O bonds ($EN_C = 2.55$, $EN_O = 3.44$; difference $=0.89$), but its linear geometry prevents an overall dipole
