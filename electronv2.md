# Unit 02: Electrons – Notes

## 1. History of Atomic Models

-   **Solid Sphere Model (John Dalton, 1803):** Proposed that atoms are indivisible particles and elements consist of identical atoms.
-   **Plum Pudding Model (J.J. Thomson, 1904):** Discovered electrons (called "corpuscles") and modeled the atom as negatively charged electrons embedded within a spherical cloud of positive charge.
-   **Nuclear Model (Ernest Rutherford, 1911):** Fired positive alpha particles at gold foil, demonstrating that atoms consist mostly of empty space with a positive charge localized in a central nucleus.
-   **Planetary Model (Niels Bohr, 1913):** Stated that electrons move in quantized orbits of fixed size and energy. Bohr later noted that electrons move more like bees around a hive, and his model worked well only for the hydrogen atom.
-   **Quantum Model (Erwin Schrödinger, 1926):** Replaced fixed orbital paths with 3D "clouds of probability" called orbitals to identify where an electron is likely to be found.

## 2. Electron Structure and Energy Levels

-   **Governing Factors of Electron Behavior:**
    -   Negative electrons are **attracted to** the positive charge of protons in the nucleus.
    -   Negative electrons are **repulsed by** the negative charge of other electrons.
    -   Electrons are most stable when residing in the **lowest possible energy state**.
-   **Principal Quantum Number (\(n\)):** Symbolizes the energy level (shell) and denotes the probable distance of an electron from the nucleus.
-   **Maximum Electrons per Shell (\(2n^2\)):** Calculated using \(2n^2\), which strictly works for the first 4 energy levels.
    -   Level 1 $((n = 1)): (2(1)^2 = 2)$ electrons.
    -   Level 2 (\(n = 2\)): \(2(2)^2 = 8\) electrons.
    -   Level 3 (\(n = 3\)): \(2(3)^2 = 18\) electrons.
    -   Level 4 (\(n = 4\)): \(2(4)^2 = 32\) electrons.
-   **Shells, Sub-shells, and Orbitals:**
    -   A **shell** describes the energy level occupied by an electron.
    -   An **orbital** describes a region within an energy level containing \(90%\) of the total electron probability surface.
    -   Every individual orbital can hold a maximum of \(2\) electrons.
    -   **\(s\) sub-shell:** Contains \(1\) spherical orbital centered around the origin of the three spatial axes.
    -   **\(p\) sub-shell:** Contains \(3\) dumbbell-shaped orbitals (\(2p_x\), \(2p_y\), \(2p_z\)) assigned to their own axes for energy levels above \(n = 1\), holding a maximum of \(6\) electrons.
    -   **\(d\) sub-shell:** Contains \(5\) orbitals ("double dumbbells" and a "dumbbell with a donut") starting at \(n = 3\), holding a maximum of \(10\) electrons.
    -   **\(f\) sub-shell:** Contains \(7\) complex orbitals starting at \(n = 4\), holding a maximum of \(14\) electrons.

## 3. Electron Configurations and Filling Rules

-   **Periodic Table Blocks:** Divided into \(s\)-block, \(p\)-block, \(d\)-block, and \(f\)-block regions corresponding to sub-shell filling. Elements within the same row (period) have the same number of electron shells.
-   **Aufbau Principle:** Orbitals fill in order of increasing energy levels from lowest to highest.
-   **Madelung Rule (\(n + \ell\) Rule):** Determines relative sub-shell energy order.
    -   The azimuthal quantum number (\(\ell\)) values correspond to sub-shells: \(s \rightarrow \ell = 0\), \(p \rightarrow \ell = 1\), \(d \rightarrow \ell = 2\), and \(f \rightarrow \ell = 3\).
    -   Sub-shells fill in order of increasing \(n + \ell\); if two sub-shells share the same \(n + \ell\) sum, the one with the lower \(n\) value fills first.
-   **Configuration Notation:** Formatted as \(n\text{sub-shell}^{\text{electrons}}\) (e.g., Helium is \(1s^2\)).
-   **Steps for Configuration:**
    1.  Count total electrons using the atomic number.
    2.  Start filling from the lowest energy level (\(1s\)) upward.
    3.  Noble gas shorthand condenses inner shell configurations (e.g., Cobalt with \(27\) electrons is \([Ar]4s^2 3d^7\)).
    4.  This predicted filling pattern strictly works for the first \(23\) elements, as transition metals exhibit experimental conflicts with standard Aufbau rules under ambient conditions.
-   **Electron Spin:** Describes the behavior of an electron within a magnetic field, taking values of \(+\frac{1}{2}\) or \(-\frac{1}{2}\). Spin direction is depicted in orbital diagrams using opposing arrows.

## 4. Valence Electrons and Lewis Dot Structures

-   **Definition:** Valence electrons are the electrons located in the \(s\) and \(p\) sub-shells of the outermost shell available for chemical bonding.
-   **Periodic Group Trends:** Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons and similar chemical properties.
    -   **Group 1 (alkali metals):** \(1\) valence electron.
    -   **Group 2 (alkaline earth metals):** \(2\) valence electrons.
    -   **Group 13:** \(3\) valence electrons.
    -   **Group 14:** \(4\) valence electrons.
    -   **Group 15:** \(5\) valence electrons.
    -   **Group 16:** \(6\) valence electrons.
    -   **Group 17 (halogens):** \(7\) valence electrons.
    -   **Group 18 (noble gases):** \(8\) valence electrons (except Helium, which has \(2\)).
-   **Lewis Dot Notations:** Represent an atom's valence electrons as dots drawn around the elemental symbol.

## 5. Wave-Particle Duality and Electromagnetic Radiation

-   **Dilemma of the Atom:** Classical electrodynamics suggested orbiting charged electrons moving in curved paths would continuously lose energy and collapse into the nucleus.
-   **Mechanics Domains:**
    -   **Classical Mechanics:** Describes macroscopic motion (projectiles, planets; Copernicus, Newton, Einstein).
    -   **Quantum Mechanics:** Handles atomic scales and wave-particle duality (Schrödinger, Heisenberg, Pauli).
-   **Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle:** It is impossible to know both the exact position and velocity of a particle simultaneously, as the act of measurement changes one or the other.
-   **Wave-Particle Duality:** Light and electrons (\(e^-\)) exhibit both wave and particle characteristics. Treating electrons as waves resolves atomic collapse: moving closer to the nucleus shortens wavelength, raising energy and maintaining distance from the nucleus.
-   **Electromagnetic Radiation Equations:**
    -   **Speed of Light:** \(c = \nu \lambda\), where \(c = 3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}\) (speed of light constant), \(\nu\) is frequency in hertz (\(\text{Hz}\) or \(\text{sec}^{-1}\)), and \(\lambda\) is wavelength in meters. Frequency and wavelength have an inverse relationship.
    -   **Photon Energy:** \(E = h \nu\), where \(E\) is energy in Joules (\(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2\)), \(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}\) (Planck's constant), and \(\nu\) is frequency in hertz (\(\text{Hz}\) or \(\text{sec}^{-1}\)). Energy and frequency are directly proportional.
    -   Long wavelength = low frequency = low energy.
    -   Short wavelength = high frequency = high energy.

## 6. Atomic Emission Spectra, Flame Tests, and Absorption

-   **Energy Quanta and Photons:** Electron jumps between energy levels involve fixed amounts of energy called quanta, and quanta of light are called photons.
-   **Hydrogen Spectral Series:** Discrete electron transitions produce distinct wavelengths:
    -   **Lyman Series:** Drops to \(n = 1\) produce ultraviolet light.
    -   **Balmer Series:** Drops to \(n = 2\) produce visible light.
    -   **Paschen Series:** Drops to \(n = 3\) produce infrared light.
    -   **Ground State:** Represents the lowest possible energy state of an electron.
-   **Emission Spectroscopy:** Spectroscopic analysis of light emitted from excited hydrogen gas produces a discrete "bright line" spectrum.
-   **Flame Tests and Fireworks:** Heating elements excites electrons, causing them to emit characteristic colors of visible light useful for identifying metals (e.g., strontium, sodium, lithium, potassium, copper) and formulating fireworks.
-   **Absorption Spectroscopy:** Occurs when light passes through a cool gas or atmosphere (such as sodium filtering the star light of HD 209458) and specific wavelengths are absorbed, creating an absorption line spectrum.
